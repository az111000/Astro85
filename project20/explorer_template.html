Where do the highest-energy particles come from?

Cosmic rays are atomic nuclei that hit Earth from space. The most energetic ones carry as much energy as a thrown baseball, packed into a single particle, and nobody knows for sure what accelerates them. If they come from particular galaxies, their arrival directions should pile up in particular spots on the sky. This page tests that with real data.

What was done

Three steps, from a starter plot to two statistical tests

Step 1 · starter

Plot where the particles came from

The starter script 20_cosmic_ray_skymap.py downloads Auger’s catalog of its 109 most energetic events and plots each arrival direction on a map of the whole sky, sized by energy.

Step 2 · prototype

Test for a pile-up near Centaurus A

Count events within 25° of Centaurus A, then compare with 1,000 shuffled skies that keep Auger’s view of the sky but contain no sources.

Step 3 · stretch

Look for a lopsided sky above 8 EeV

Download Auger’s larger public dataset (2,465 events above 8 EeV) and measure whether more particles arrive from one side.

Step 2 · Thursday prototype

Is the bunch around Centaurus A real, or luck?

Auger doesn’t see the whole sky equally: it mostly sees the south. But the Earth spins, so every east–west direction (right ascension) gets the same viewing time. A fair “no sources” sky keeps each event’s north–south position (declination) and gives it a random right ascension. The p-value is the share of those shuffled skies with at least as many events in the circle as the real sky.

A warning about the slider. If you try many circle sizes and report only the best one, you will fool yourself. This is called the look-elsewhere effect. The fair answer is the one chosen before looking: 25° around Centaurus A gives p ≈ 0.31 in the Python analysis. Across every radius from 5° to 60°, the smallest p is about 0.11, still not significant.

Step 3 · Stretch goal

Do more particles arrive from one side of the sky?

In 2017 Auger announced that, above 8 EeV, about 6% more particles arrive from one direction than from the opposite one, pointing away from our galaxy’s centre. Here the same test runs on Auger’s public dataset, about a tenth of their events. We count events in each 30° slice of right ascension and fit a wave, 1 + r·cos(RA − phase). The chance of a wave this big from a smooth sky is P = exp(−N r²/4).

What I found

Hints pointing the right way, but not enough data to be sure

Centaurus A

13 events within 25°, where chance gives 11.2, so p ≈ 0.31. With only 109 events, a small excess like this is easily luck.

Dipole above 8 EeV

A 5.8% ± 2.8% wave at right ascension 144°, matching Auger’s 4.7% within the errors. On its own it has a 12% chance of being noise.

What would settle it

About ten times more events. Auger’s full dataset shows the dipole at 5.2σ. This analysis skips Auger’s small corrections for weather, detector growth and Earth’s magnetic field.
